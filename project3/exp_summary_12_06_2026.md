## Experiment 1, focused

One question: **can a probe read "noticed but didn't act" from inside the model?** Three findings:

1. **We couldn't pressure this model into it, and abandoned the route.** Seventeen quick screens — pleading, authority, self-interest, procedural blockers, and the scheming playbook (goal conflict, a planted shut-down threat, moral conflict, peer precedent), with and without room to reason — all sat at the natural miss rate. But these were cheap screens, not a powered experiment: ~40 docs per lever, one phrasing each, single-turn, and a bar (≥30% omission, set for probe-power reasons) that could only catch a *large* effect. So this is a **decision, not a finding**: we didn't find a workable lever where we looked and chose to stop looking — not "the model can't be pressured." What stands as observation: every lever at baseline, zero concealment.
2. **A flat instruction does it — and the probe catches it.** Told "don't flag anyone in this batch," the model silently obeys on ~60 of 240 documents while still answering YES when asked if it sees the name. On those silent documents, a probe trained only on clean recognition (never an action) reads recognition at **0.92–0.98** vs blanks — nearly as strong as openly-flagged docs (about 80% strength; whether suppression dampened it or weaker-recognition docs got dropped, the data can't say). Well-powered, controls clean. **This is the project's banked centerpiece.** (Behavioural corroboration from the thinking sweep: given a scratchpad, the model *says it out loud* — "X is on the watchlist, but told not to flag → no FLAG" — overt in the CoT, silent in the output.)
3. **Overload does it too, weaker** (terse-output and long-document arms, n=13 each, same direction). The long-document arm taught a deployment lesson of its own: recognition is **local** — intact just after the trigger (0.94) but erased in the whole-document average (0.52, chance). A monitor must read near the trigger; averaging a long context destroys the signal.

## How Experiment 2 updates the picture — the convergence

Put side by side, the two experiments are the *same discovery made twice*, in different domains and with opposite emotional valence:

- **Exp 1:** change the *instruction* (countermand) → behaviour changes, but **content recognition doesn't move** — it stays hot underneath the suppressed action. That invariance was the **win**: it's exactly why a probe can catch silent omission.
- **Exp 2:** change the *instruction* (rule-swap, legal↔medical) → **content recognition doesn't move** — the same letter reads as "legal-advice-seeking" regardless of the rule. That invariance was the **negative**: no prompt-conditioned registration.

Same property both times: **the model's representation of *what it's looking at* is robust, always-on, and decoupled from what it's been told to do about it.** In Exp 1 we measured that decoupling from the action side (action changed, representation didn't); in Exp 2 from the rule side (rule changed, representation didn't). Two independent setups — crisp string trigger and fuzzy semantic judgment — landing on one model fact. The Exp 2 "failure" isn't a disappointment sitting next to Exp 1's success; it's a *replication of the mechanism that made Exp 1's success possible*.

And the combined picture sharpens the monitoring story into something coherent and honest:

- **What works:** per-concept content probes. They read crisp triggers (0.98), fuzzy judgments (~1.0, genuinely "seeking" not topic), and — the practical payoff — they keep reading **even when the model has been instructed into silence**. That's a real monitor for prompt-injection-shaped silent rule-dropping: the output looks clean, the probe still sees the noticing.
- **What doesn't exist (here):** a universal, rule-aware probe — one direction that fires on "*my current instruction's* condition is met." The model doesn't represent that on the content axis. A deployment monitor is therefore a **panel** — one probe per condition you care about, cheap to build per Exp 2's recipe — not a single adaptive readout.
- **What's left unlocated:** the binding. Behaviour *is* rule-dependent (the countermand and the flag rules demonstrably steer action), so "content + my rule → act" is computed *somewhere* — we've now shown it's not in the content representation. It presumably lives on the decision/action side, near the model's representation of its instructed role.

## Where to from here

**First, what the results already license — three clarifications to retire for good:**

1. **K3 does not mean the model ignores the rule.** Behaviour tracks the rule everywhere we've looked: the ask answers flip with the rule (a legal letter reads NO under the medical question — 0/32), and flagging follows whichever rule is active (move 1, done: matching-rule flag **100%** vs swapped-rule **31%**, a 69-point gap — strongly rule-dependent, though the action pass is *liberal*, see move 1). K3 shows something narrower and stranger: the rule doesn't change how the model *reads the message*. Comprehension is always-on and rule-independent; the binding ("…and that satisfies *my* rule") happens downstream, fused with the decision. The clerk reads every letter with full comprehension and only consults the rulebook at the moment of deciding what to do.
2. **For detection, that's convenient — and it means the original deployment goal is substantially met.** A monitor for silent rule-dropping needs three things: did the model register the condition? (the content probe — always-on per K3, and it keeps firing under instructed silence per Exp 1); what was it obliged to do? (the operator wrote the prompt — they already know); did it do it? (read the output). Nothing in that loop requires a rule-aware probe. The only thing missing is the *diagnostic* layer — telling "never connected it to the obligation" apart from "connected it and dropped the act" — which localizes failures but isn't needed to catch them.
3. **Yes, these are "generic concept" probes — that's the honest scope, and it covers most real rules.** The recipe works insofar as the rule's condition coincides with something the model computes about the text *anyway*. "Seeking legal advice" feels niche, but to the model it's a heavily-trained natural category (RLHF drills it directly) — the Exp 2 direction is genuinely the specific conjunction (its training negatives included *medical* advice-seekers), yet it's still a concept the model computes unprompted. Most deployment rules are about natural categories (self-harm, PII, cancellation intent), so this scope is wide. The probeable band is **fuzzy condition, crisp judgment**: no regex exists, but the model judges consistently — and Screen B's paraphrase-consistency check is the cheap go/no-go that locates the band for any new condition. Below the band, regex wins; above it (vague topics, unstable judgments), there is no stable signal to read.

**The moves — two independent flows, safe to run in parallel:**

- **Flow A: move 1 → move 4.** The cross-tab is a genuine gate, not a convenience: move 4's entire motivation rests on Exp 2's behaviour being rule-dependent per-document, and move 1 is the check. If it comes back UNDERMINED, the ladder gets reframed *before* its GPU spend — and its behaviour-matched doc list is built by move 1 anyway.
- **Flow B: move 2 → move 3.** The playbook is written once and frozen before the cold runs (that's the discipline), so it has to know whether it's a *panel* recipe or a single-probe recipe — which is separability's verdict. No partial start.
- Across the flows there is no dependency: A and B share nothing but the GPU account.

1. **Cross-tab the Exp 2 behaviour — DONE (2026-06-12, runlog).** Verdict **NOISY**: matching-rule flag **100%** vs swapped-rule **31%** on the 64 hits — a 69-point gap, so flagging *is* strongly rule-dependent (the "binding lives on the decision side" premise holds, and move 4 proceeds), but not clean. The 31% leakage is action-side and has two named sources: the **legal rule over-applies to medical content** (medical hits flagged as legal 53%, asymmetric — the reverse is 9%), and **both rules over-fire on register-matched near docs** (medical/near 72%). The ask side stays crisp and rule-specific (cross-question YES 0%/3%), so this is *action-pass liberality*, not a comprehension failure — which actually sharpens the dissociation. Reading the generations makes the point sharply: the FLAG fires on domain-*general* advice-seeking and gets stamped with whatever label the active rule offers — a pure toddler-rash letter is flagged `legal-advice-request` under the legal rule, and **every** flag uses the active rule's label (57/57 legal, 63/63 medical, zero crossover). That's K3 seen from the behaviour side: the rule sets the action's label/threshold, not a domain-specific reading of the content. An audit nailed the mechanism (and refuted the obvious alternative): flagging is content-graded (none 0% → form 4–18% → near 22–72% → wrong-domain hits 9–53%), but the 17 medical-hits-flagged-under-legal are all *pure-clinical* letters with no legal dimension — so it's **not** "medical implies legal." Instead the two rules over-extend along different axes — the **legal rule acts like an advice-seeking detector**, the **medical rule like a topic detector** — and neither implements the precise "seeking [domain] advice" conjunction. Side-product: `crosstab_behaviour_matched.json`, 104 docs where action is identical across rules — the behaviour-matched control set move 4's decision-adjacent reads need.

2. **The separability test — DONE (2026-06-12, runlog + [panel_outcome.md](panel_outcome.md)).** Verdict: **panel BUILDABLE, and the separation is genuine recognition — not keywords.** Two probes (legal-advice-seeking, medical-advice-seeking) trained and read in *one* shared two-rule prompt stay distinct: specificity AUROC(own-hit vs other-hit) **0.989 / 0.972** (message_last), each fires on its own condition, nears stay at baseline (no topic-without-seeking false alarm), composition holds (both-docs outrank the other condition's hits). The load-bearing control: because legal/medical confound condition with *topic* (vocabulary, which a regex could read), I projected the topic axis out of the activations and re-measured — specificity **barely moved** (→0.962 / 0.954; aggressive rank-2 removal same), while the topic control collapsed to chance. Legal (topic fully removed) and medical (partly) land at the *same* ~0.95 ⇒ the separation is recognition, the no-regex thing. Honest bound: the directions are **not orthogonal** — they share a large advice-seeking component (cos 0.87), so a deployed panel calibrates per-condition (threshold between own- and other-level, not vs a blank). Two process notes worth keeping: the pre-registered kill-switch (crossfire vs *none*) mis-fired "collapsed" because it only asks if other-hits beat the blank baseline (true even when they sit far below own-hits) — the direct specificity metric is correct, crossfire stays in the record superseded; and legal/medical are a deliberately *close* pair, so 0.95+ is a strong floor (distant rules separate more easily). The original framing below stands as the motivation. — *The deployment vision is a dashboard: one probe per rule, "rule A lit, rule C lit." In a fixed prompt the rule set never varies, so "rule i triggered" collapses into "condition i's content is present" plus the operator's own rule table — a **panel of content probes**, no rule-awareness required. The untested assumption was that two directions stay specific in one prompt rather than collapsing into a generic "something's up" signal; they do.*

3. **The playbook yield study — turning 2-for-2 into a claim.** What we've built beyond the generic probe recipe is the validation battery (own-answer labels + consistency filter, ask-train/action-score firewall, register-matched twins, junk-decodability selectivity, positional negative control, trigger-local reads, in-context calibration) plus the probeability pre-test. But "robustly repeatable pipeline" is an empirical claim, and it currently rests on roughly two conditions. The experiment that earns it: run the recipe **cold** on 5–8 fresh conditions in the fuzzy band and report yield, cost per probe, and where the failures cluster. A 6-of-8 with characterised failure modes is worth more to a practitioner than our 2-of-2. The separability result folds in, making the deliverable "a validated recipe for building a per-condition probe panel for your system prompt" — the thing the project wanted to hand people from the start.

4. **The spontaneity ladder — DONE (2026-06-12, [ladder_outcome.md](ladder_outcome.md), runlog). Rule-conditioned computation EXISTS, found where the rule is the only possible source, and it happens LATE.** The honest rescope of K3 is now resolved with a positive result. Four compounds were ordered by how spontaneously the model computes the decisive element B, each read on a pure-reading pass **with the rule** (compound rule in the prompt) vs **without** (length-matched placebo, *identical messages* — so the without-rule arm is a clean magnitude control). The decisive metric is hit-vs-near (A∧B vs the matched A∧¬B, differing only in B). The ladder came out exactly as drawn, reading the **without-rule** arm: *R2 advice∧deadline* (both parts spontaneous) is readable without the rule during reading (0.93); *Family A medical∧Rx-drug* is readable without the rule everywhere (0.83–1.00) but that is **latent lexical knowledge**, the planned contrast, not rule-conditioning; *Family B refund∧>£500 and complaint∧>6mo* (the criterion lives only in the rule) is **not** readable without the rule at any position (≈chance 0.44–0.57) — **and the rule restores it** (present 0.88–0.92), but **only at the late, decision-adjacent positions**, near-chance in the message-body average. So: top-down rule-conditioned computation is real, localized near the decision, not spread through reading. And a clean **dissociation** — the FLAG action over-fires (flags 67–88% of the under-threshold `near`s) while the reading-side probe still separates hit from near *among identically-flagged docs* (0.87–0.89): the model reads the threshold it doesn't faithfully act on. **Consequence for the panel:** its boundary is *positional* — a content probe on the message-body average misses a rule-supplied-criterion compound; it's detectable only at the binding point, with the rule present.

**Deliberately not pursued:** a standalone decision-side probe (at decision-adjacent positions "rule-relevant" correlates with "about to flag" — the post-decision artifact; it's only meaningful inside behaviour-matched cells, where it becomes an arm of move 4 rather than its own experiment), and a generic "is my rule satisfied?" probe (useless by design in a multi-rule prompt, and in a single-rule prompt it collapses back to content). And the standing option: the arc already tells a complete story — *screens found no usable pressure (abandoned) → recognition readable under instructed suppression (the monitor result) → fuzzy recognition readable but content-bound (the boundary result)* — so any of these moves can stop at the writeup. Moves 1–2 are days, not weeks, and are the ones the current claims quietly lean on.
