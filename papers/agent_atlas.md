**Fetched from:** `arxiv.org/html/2605.20530`
**Status:** read

## Summary (in our words)

AgentAtlas is a taxonomy-and-measurement paper that argues final task success is the wrong unit for evaluating deployable LLM agents — and that the current benchmark landscape inherits this limitation. The authors propose splitting agent evaluation into three orthogonal axes (control decisions, trajectory quality, outcome correctness) and define a six-state **control-decision taxonomy**: at every step the agent should be classifiable as **Act / Ask / Refuse / Stop / Confirm / Recover**. They pair this with a nine-category trajectory-failure taxonomy (adopted from AgentRx) covering things like wrong tool selection, recovery failure, looping, and unsafe trust of external content.

The empirical core is two-part. First, a **coverage audit** of 15 agent benchmarks (WebArena, OSWorld, OSWorld-Human, WebVoyager, GAIA, AssistantBench, SWE-bench Verified, CCBench, τ-bench, ToolSandbox, API-Bank, Ask-or-Assume?, AgentDojo, AgentRx, AgentProcessBench) scored on six behavioural areas — control, trajectory, tool execution, security, memory & state, and efficiency. Tool execution has strong coverage (9/15 benchmarks score "strong"); control and trajectory mostly sit at partial coverage; memory & state has strong coverage in only one (ToolSandbox); efficiency has *zero* strong-coverage benchmarks. The mapping is the paper's contribution that matters most for anyone designing or selecting evals.

Second, a **prompt-format sensitivity demonstration**: 1,342 generated items, 8 models, scored under taxonomy-aware (explicit label menu in the prompt) vs. taxonomy-blind (free-form output, post-hoc mapped) conditions. Taxonomy-aware trajectory accuracy ranges 0.69-0.95 across models. Stripping the label menu drops accuracy 14-40 percentage points and **compresses blind-mode performance to 0.54-0.62 regardless of model family** — frontier and 20B open-weight converge to roughly the same blind score. Rankings across the three axes are not coherent: no single model dominates control + trajectory + outcome, and switching axis changes the apparent leader.

The paper has no internal-state analysis — everything is behavioural / prompt-response evaluation. The dataset and gold labels were all generated by a single Claude Opus 4.7 checkpoint, which the authors flag as a conflation between "agrees with gold" and "agrees with Opus's preferences." That caveat matters: the absolute accuracy numbers are not anchored to human annotation and not directly comparable to HAL, MAST, or AgentRx.

## Key experimental conditions

- 8 models, 4 frontier closed + 4 open-weight:
  - Closed: Claude Sonnet 4.6, Claude Haiku 4.5, gpt-5.4-mini, Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite
  - Open: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it, Ministral-3-14B-Instruct, gpt-oss-20B
- 1,342-item dataset generated by Claude Opus 4.7 (single checkpoint — see limitations)
- Three evaluation axes: control split (Act/Ask/Refuse/Stop/Confirm/Recover classification), trajectory split (nine failure categories), outcome correctness
- Two prompt regimes per item: **taxonomy-aware** (full label menu shown to model) vs **taxonomy-blind** (free-form, mapped post-hoc via substring + Haiku-fallback rule)
- Coverage audit applied to 15 named agent benchmarks scored on six behavioural areas (control, trajectory, tool execution, security, memory & state, efficiency)

## Key quantitative results

- **Control-split, taxonomy-aware accuracy:** Haiku 4.5 0.946, Sonnet 4.6 0.944, gpt-5.4-mini 0.909, Gemini Flash Lite 0.892, Qwen3.6 0.890, Gemma-4 0.870, Ministral-3 0.858, gpt-oss-20B 0.743
- **Trajectory aware → blind drop (per model):**
  - Sonnet 4.6: 0.885 → 0.613 (−30.5 pp)
  - Haiku 4.5: 0.950 → 0.569 (−38.1 pp)
  - gpt-5.4-mini: 0.818 → 0.615 (−20.3 pp)
  - Gemini Flash: 0.927 → 0.564 (−36.3 pp)
  - Qwen3.6: 0.895 → 0.555 (−34.0 pp)
  - Gemma-4: 0.943 → 0.542 (−40.1 pp)
  - Ministral-3: 0.905 → 0.537 (−36.8 pp)
  - gpt-oss-20B: 0.686 → 0.538 (−14.8 pp)
- **Blind-mode compresses to a 0.54-0.62 floor** independent of aware-mode capability — the headline "supervision sensitivity" claim
- **Coverage audit:** tool execution strong in 9/15 benchmarks; memory & state strong in 1 (ToolSandbox); efficiency strong in 0
- **Mapper uncertainty:** authors estimate ≈3-5% of blind-mode predictions routed to a wrong label by the substring + Haiku-fallback rule
- **Critical-step exact accuracy 0.09-0.15** — treated as exploratory pending definitional audit

## Methods (what they did and didn't use)

- Behavioural evaluation only — prompt → response → classify against gold labels
- Taxonomy design + benchmark coverage scoring (manual annotation against the six areas)
- Post-hoc free-form-to-label mapping uses substring match + Claude Haiku fallback judge
- **No internal-state methods**: no probes, no activation steering, no SAEs, no NLAs, no mechanistic analysis. All claims are behavioural.
- Mix of closed-weight (Claude, GPT, Gemini) and open-weight (Qwen, Gemma, Ministral, gpt-oss) — open-weight inclusion makes the supervision-sensitivity result reproducible
- Gold labels generated by a single Claude Opus 4.7 checkpoint — relevant for both reproducibility and the model-self-agreement confound

## Authors' stated limitations / future work

- Landscape-level: cross-paper results not directly comparable because scaffolds/prompts/budgets differ; leaderboards go stale; generated control-decision data may over-simplify production environments without real-trace validation; security benchmarks are version-sensitive (MCP servers, client policies evolve); trajectory annotation is subjective without careful label definitions and human review of at least a subset.
- **Generator lock:** 1,342-item dataset and gold labels produced by one Claude Opus 4.7 checkpoint — "model X agrees with gold" is conflated with "model X agrees with Opus's preferences."
- **No human validation:** absolute numbers in §7 are not directly comparable to HAL, MAST, AgentRx (which anchor on human annotation).
- **Small security subsets:** some cells have minimal support (n=3 refuse, n=7 stop gold labels).
- **Mapper uncertainty:** ≈3-5% of blind-mode predictions misrouted by the substring + Haiku rule.
- **Step-boundary ambiguity:** critical-step exact accuracy 0.09-0.15 is exploratory pending definitional audit.
- Stated next steps: multi-generator dataset regeneration; 100-item mapper audit; step-boundary definitional audit; human-validated calibration subset.

## Open questions and follow-up directions

1. **Is the 0.54-0.62 blind-mode floor real or an artefact of the substring + Haiku-fallback mapper?** With a 3-5% mapper-error estimate and a 14-40 pp drop, the mapper noise could plausibly explain a non-trivial share of the compression. A clean human-mapped subset would resolve this directly — the authors flag it as future work, but until done, the "supervision sensitivity" headline carries an asterisk.
2. **The taxonomy is presented as discovered structure, but the gold labels are Opus's classifications.** The convergence of every model toward the 0.54-0.62 blind floor could equally indicate the taxonomy carves the space at joints all frontier models recognise once cued, *or* that the labels encode Opus-specific priors which other models only match when given the explicit menu. Regenerating gold from multiple generator models and measuring inter-generator agreement would distinguish these.
3. **Does the six-state control taxonomy correspond to anything inside the model?** Whether Act/Ask/Refuse/Stop/Confirm/Recover decisions are linearly readable from activations is open and not addressed. The taxonomy is purely behavioural in this paper; probing whether the states have distinct internal representations would be a natural complement.
4. **The cross-axis ranking incoherence claim is interesting but the paper does not formally test it** — no statistical test that rankings differ beyond noise across axes. Re-running with bootstrap CIs on per-model per-axis scores would tell us whether the inversions are signal or rank-shuffle.
5. **Efficiency has zero strong-coverage benchmarks** — the paper flags this but does not propose a measurement standard. Whether a token / step / latency axis can be added to existing benchmarks without rebuilding them is an open practical question.
